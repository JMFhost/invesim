Premium Harvest

A guide distilled from the book Premium Harvest: How Smart Investors Stop Gambling and Start Collecting by Jesse Pham. InveSim uses virtual money only — this guide is education, not financial advice.

Most options traders are glorified gamblers playing a rigged game they don't understand. They buy lottery tickets disguised as call options, chase ten-baggers, and wonder why their accounts evaporate. Meanwhile a small group of investors quietly collects premium checks every month and sleeps soundly. The difference: one group pays rent, the other collects it. This book teaches you to be the house — selling premium systematically, managing risk ruthlessly, and letting time decay and probability do the work. Boring and profitable beats exciting and broke.

1. Be the house, not the gambler

There are exactly two teams in options: premium buyers and premium sellers. Roughly 90% of options traders lose money — not because options are inherently risky, but because 90% of traders are buying lottery tickets. A casino doesn't make money by winning every hand; it has a small edge on every hand and lets probability work over thousands of transactions. Option sellers do the same. The statistic that "90% of options expire worthless" is not a warning — it is a roadmap: when an option expires worthless, the seller keeps the premium.

The myths: options are not inherently risky — selling a covered call on stock you own is less risky than holding the stock alone. You don't need a math PhD — you need common sense and a calculator. Options are not only for short-term trading — the best income strategies run for weeks and months. And selling premium is not gambling — it is a business with known maximum gains, known risks, and a mathematical edge.

Three principles govern everything in the book: (1) never sell options on assets you don't understand or wouldn't want to own; (2) position sizing matters more than strike selection; (3) premium is income, not lottery winnings — reinvest it, don't gamble it.

Feel the difference — it is the whole book in one lesson.

2. Covered calls: the cash machine

You own 100 shares of a stock at $50. You sell a call at a $55 strike, 30 days out, and collect $150. Three outcomes: the stock stays below $55 — the option expires worthless, you keep shares and premium, and you sell another call next month (3% for the month, ~36% annualized). The stock rises above $55 — your shares are called away at $55, you keep $500 of capital gains plus $150 premium, a 13% month, and you missed some upside you never had any right to expect. The stock crashes — you lose less than a plain shareholder, because you banked $150 of premium cushion.

Strike selection: if you love the stock and want to keep it, sell strikes further out of the money to reduce assignment odds. If you'd happily sell at a modest profit, sell closer strikes for more premium. Never sell at-the-money or in-the-money strikes for ongoing income — you'll give up appreciation and get called away almost immediately. Monthly expirations are the sweet spot for most people: meaningful premium, clean cycle, manageable time commitment. Rolling — closing a decayed option and selling a new one for a net credit — can boost returns, but most of the time the best move is to do nothing and let time decay work.

Mistakes to avoid: selling calls on stocks you want to keep forever and then crying when they're called away; chasing fat premium on garbage stocks (high premium means high volatility means high risk); ignoring tax consequences of early assignment; and assuming retirement-account rules are the same everywhere.

Try it in InveSim: Own at least 100 shares of a stock, then write a call above the current price in the Options system. Writing a call against shares you own reproduces the covered-call economics (premium in, upside capped at the strike by the cash settlement), without the assignment mechanics. Run it monthly: write 30-day calls, collect premium, and compare your total return against simply holding the same stock.

3. Protective puts: insurance that actually works

Markets crash roughly once a decade, and every time, unprotected investors act shocked. Diversification doesn't save you when everything falls together. A protective put is literal insurance: buy a put on stock you own, and your losses are floored at the strike price. Own Microsoft at $350, buy a $330 put for $500: if the stock falls to $300, your put is worth $3,000 and your net loss is $2,000 instead of $5,000. If the stock rises, the put expires worthless and you're out the insurance premium — exactly like car insurance you never claimed on.

Use protection selectively. Young investors with decades ahead probably don't need constant insurance — the cumulative premium cost exceeds the benefit. Near retirement, holding large unrealized gains you don't want taxed by selling, or facing a known high-risk period: protection makes sense. Buy puts 5–10% out of the money, typically quarterly (90 days) for ongoing coverage, and buy them during calm periods when they're cheap — insurance is cheapest when you feel you need it least and most expensive after the crash has started. Combine a protective put with a covered call and you have a collar: the call premium pays for the put, capping both your upside and your downside, sometimes at zero net cost.

4. Cash-secured puts: get paid to wait

If you want to buy a stock at $90 and it's trading at $100, why set a limit order and earn nothing while you wait? Sell a put at the $90 strike and collect premium for the commitment. If the stock drops, you buy at your target price with a lower effective cost basis (strike minus premium). If it never drops, you keep the premium and sell another put. Either way you win — provided you follow the one non-negotiable rule: only sell puts on stocks you genuinely want to own at the strike price.

Sell strikes 5–15% out of the money at prices that represent real value, on quality companies with solid fundamentals — not meme stocks with juicy premium that will leave you owning garbage. Use 30–60 day expirations. Keep the cash actually reserved: a $50 put means $5,000 available, no exceptions. Never commit more than 10–15% of your portfolio to a single put position. And when you get assigned, don't panic-sell — assignment is the strategy working. You now own a stock you wanted, at a price you chose, and you can start selling covered calls against it.

The premium-collection and obligation-management practice is real; the share-acquisition step itself is concept-only here — after the settlement, buy the shares at the lower price yourself with the premium you banked, and you've reproduced the full cycle.

5. Risk management: how not to blow up

Most traders fail not from bad strategy but from no risk management: positions too large, correlations ignored, losses nursed until they become catastrophes. The rules:

  • Position sizing: never risk more than 1–2% of your account on a single trade. The Kelly Criterion suggests mathematically "optimal" sizes, but it assumes you know your exact probabilities — use fractional Kelly (divide by 2 or 4) or stick to the 1–2% rule.
  • Correlation: four tech stocks are not diversification. Spread across sectors, market caps, and asset classes, or one sector correction takes every position at once.
  • Cut losses: if a sold option loses more than 50% of the premium you collected, close it. Take the manageable loss before it becomes catastrophic.
  • Take profits: if you've captured 75% of the maximum gain with time left, close it and free the capital. Don't risk a winning trade for the last few dollars.
  • Black Swans: crashes, flash crashes, and crises happen more often than models predict. No single event should be able to destroy your account — that is what sizing, diversification, and cash reserves are for.
  • Portfolio construction: allocate 20–30% of capital to active options positions, no more than 15–20% of that to any single position, across 5–10 underlyings in different sectors.

Before every trade, answer seven questions: account size, dollars at risk, is it under 2%, total exposure across positions, diversification, exit plan if wrong, profit plan if right. If you can't answer all seven, don't trade.

6. Advanced strategies (for later, not now)

Once the core strategies are second nature, four advanced structures build on them. Credit spreads (bull put spreads, bear call spreads) cap your risk by buying a further-out-of-the-money option against the one you sell — less premium, defined maximum loss. The sweet spot is roughly the 50-delta short / 25-delta long, collecting about a third of the spread width. Calendar spreads sell a near-term option and buy a longer-term one at the same strike, profiting from faster near-term decay and rising volatility when prices stay stable. Diagonal spreads add a directional bias to the calendar by using different strikes. Iron condors combine two credit spreads to profit when a stock stays inside a wide range — high win probability, but losses tend to be the maximum, so you need a 70–75% win rate.

The warning matters more than the strategies: complexity is not sophistication. Most traders would be better off staying with covered calls, protective puts, and cash-secured puts. Advanced structures might add 10–20% to returns when executed perfectly and destroy 50%+ when executed poorly. Master the core strategies for at least a year before touching credit spreads; another year before iron condors.

7. The psychology of not sabotaging yourself

Your brain is your worst enemy. Loss aversion makes losses feel twice as painful as equal gains feel good — so you hold losers too long and cut winners too early. Greed makes you oversize "sure things." Confirmation bias filters out bad news about your positions. Overconfidence strikes after winning streaks and makes you abandon discipline. Recency bias makes you fight the last war. Anchoring fixates you on entry prices that no longer matter. Herd mentality pulls you into bubbles and panics. Regret aversion paralyzes you into doing nothing. Revenge trading turns a $500 loss into a $2,000 loss.

The cure is mechanical rules decided in advance: exit points, profit targets, position sizes, and a mandatory 24–72 hour cooling-off period after any loss. Successful trading is boring — the same strategies on the same kinds of stocks, month after month. If boredom makes you seek excitement in your trading, get your excitement somewhere that doesn't involve financial risk. Keep a trading journal: every trade, every deviation from plan, every emotional state. The journal forces you to confront the reality that your win rate is lower and your losses bigger than you remember.

8. Adapting to market conditions

The same strategy does not work the same way in every market. Bull markets: sell covered calls further out of the money to preserve upside; cash-secured puts can be more aggressive since assignment is unlikely; don't abandon protection just because everything is rising — bull markets end violently. Bear markets: sell calls closer to the price for more premium cushion; be very selective with puts, only on quality names at steep discounts; collars and dividend payers help. Sideways markets: the ideal income environment — sell calls at the top of the range and puts at the bottom, repeatedly, and let time decay pay you. Volatility: when the VIX is below 15, premiums are thin and protection is cheap; when it spikes above 30, premiums explode and selling is most profitable — precisely when it feels most terrifying. Earnings announcements and Fed meetings spike implied volatility; selling into that elevated premium captures extra income at the cost of event risk.

9. Building your options income empire

Wealth comes from systems, not individual trades. Build a watch list of 20–50 quality companies you understand, would own, and whose options are liquid — everything else is off limits. Run a trading calendar: monthly cycles tied to expirations, one review per day, changes only on scheduled days unless a stop or target is hit. Keep a trading journal and review it monthly: did you follow your rules, which strategies worked, were losses bad luck or bad execution, how did you adapt to conditions? Scale gradually — add breadth (more underlyings) before size, keep risk percentages constant as the account grows, and keep 20–30% of your options allocation in cash reserve for volatility spikes. Compounding does the rest: 2% a month is roughly 27% a year, turning $50,000 into over $500,000 in ten years — if you don't withdraw the profits and don't blow up the account. You don't need to quit your job to do this; a few hours a week of systematic execution is the whole business.
