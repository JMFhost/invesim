An Actual Investor

A guide distilled from the book An Actual Investor by Jesse Pham. InveSim uses virtual money only — this guide is education, not financial advice.

This book exists for one reason: most people approaching the stock market are about to lose money, and the financial industry profits from keeping it that way. Its message is blunt — day trading is a wealth-transfer machine, the apps and media around it are designed to keep you trading, and the only reliable way to build wealth is to buy good businesses at reasonable prices and hold them long enough for compounding to work. Boring beats exciting. Patience beats intelligence. Time in the market beats timing the market.

1. The slaughter: what you are up against

Between 80% and 95% of day traders lose money. Only about 13% stay profitable after six months; the one-year success rate is roughly 3%, and the five-year rate is about 1%. During the pandemic trading boom, retail traders collectively lost over $1.14 billion. These are not odds — they are the predictable output of a system where your counterparty is a high-frequency algorithm, a quantitative hedge fund, or a market maker that understands your behavior better than you do.

Every time you chase a breakout or panic-sell a dip, you behave exactly as those systems predict. Financial media exists to keep you emotionally engaged enough to keep trading — if it wanted you wealthy, every show would be five minutes long: buy quality, hold for decades, turn off the television.

2. The dopamine dealers

Modern trading apps are engineered dopamine delivery systems: confetti, notifications, green arrows, and social features that celebrate wins and bury losses. "Free" trades are paid for by payment for order flow — your broker sells your orders to firms that trade ahead of you, so you get slightly worse prices on every transaction. Volatility triggers more notifications because volatility triggers impulsive decisions. The system needs you to keep trading; a customer who quits is worthless, and a customer who keeps coming back with fresh money is invaluable.

3. The mathematics of stupidity

Losses and gains are not symmetric. Lose 50% and you need a 100% gain to break even. Lose 75% and you need 300%. Lose 90% and you need 900%. That asymmetry is why professionals obsess over risk management while amateurs obsess over potential profits.

Buying options stacks several bets at once: right direction, far enough, fast enough, before expiry, against time decay that works against you every single day. And frequency itself is a tax: spreads on every trade plus short-term tax treatment mean an active trader needs spectacular returns just to match a patient investor's ordinary ones. Market timing requires two correct decisions — when to sell and when to buy back — and missing just the 10 best days over 20 years can cut your returns by more than half.

Compounding is the other side of the math. At 10% a year — roughly the long-run S&P 500 average — $10,000 becomes about $174,000 in 30 years. At 8% it becomes about $100,000; at 12%, about $300,000. Invest $10,000 a year for 30 years at 10% and you end up over $1.8 million. The math rewards patience and punishes impatience with total indifference to your feelings.

4. What actually makes money: own businesses

A stock is not a lottery ticket or a video-game token. It is a fractional ownership stake in a real business with real customers, real products, and real profits. Warren Buffett built his fortune buying excellent businesses at reasonable prices and doing nothing exciting for years — reading annual reports, judging competitive advantages, and letting compounding do the work.

Investing is positive-sum: businesses grow, economies grow, and patient owners share in that growth. Trading is zero-sum: your gain is another trader's loss, minus costs. The average holding period has collapsed from over eight years in the 1960s to under one year today — and individual returns have suffered for it. You cannot have both excitement and wealth. Choose.

5. How to evaluate a stock

Start with the business, not the chart. Then read the three financial statements:

  • Income statement — revenue growth (is the business expanding?) and profit margins (how efficiently do sales become profits?). Growth without profit is meaningless; temporary earnings dips may just be investment in future growth.
  • Balance sheet — what the company owns versus what it owes. Heavy debt can bankrupt a profitable business in a downturn; strong cash reserves buy flexibility to survive recessions and seize opportunities.
  • Cash flow statement — actual cash generated, which is much harder to fake than reported earnings. Free cash flow — cash left after operating expenses and necessary capital spending — is the most important metric for long-term investors. It funds dividends, buybacks, and growth.

On valuation: the P/E ratio tells you how many years of current earnings you are paying for. The P/B ratio compares price to net assets. But valuation only means something alongside quality — paying a fair price for an excellent business beats paying a cheap price for a mediocre one, because excellent businesses become more valuable while mediocre ones stagnate.

6. Moats: the castle and the idiots at the gate

An economic moat is a sustainable competitive advantage that protects profit margins for years. The book covers five kinds:

  • Brand power — only valuable when customers pay premium prices because of the brand (Coca-Cola, Apple). Recognition without pricing power is worthless.
  • Network effects — the service gets more valuable as more people use it (payment networks, marketplaces). Growth feeds growth, and competitors cannot replicate the user base overnight.
  • Switching costs — enterprise software, where retraining and migration make leaving painful. Artificial lock-in without real value fails the moment contracts expire.
  • Cost advantages — scale, technology, or logistics that let a company undercut rivals while staying profitable (Walmart, AWS). Temporary cost edges disappear; structural ones endure.
  • Regulatory advantages — licenses, regulated monopolies, patents. Patents expire, so drug makers must keep innovating.

Two more tests of quality: buybacks create value only when shares are repurchased below intrinsic value — otherwise they are financial engineering. Dividends must be sustainable: unusually high yields often signal an expected cut, while wide-moat businesses can grow dividends for decades on consistent free cash flow. And management quality matters most of all — great managers allocate capital intelligently and strengthen moats; poor ones waste it and destroy even strong positions. Moats are not permanent: they erode without continuous investment, so ownership means ongoing attention, not permanent autopilot.

Try it in InveSim: Moats are qualitative, so practice reading their footprints: persistent high net margins, high ROE, and steady dividend growth on a stock's page. 7. Patient money beats stupid money

Markets transfer wealth from people who cannot wait to people who can. The research is consistent: buy-and-hold investors beat active traders after fees, taxes, and transaction costs — not because they pick better stocks, but because they avoid the systematic drag of frequent trading and emotional decisions. Most market gains arrive on a handful of days each year; miss them and your returns collapse.

Successful investors think like owners, hold through volatility, and treat downturns as sales on quality businesses. Unsuccessful traders think like gamblers, chase the next hot tip, and buy high and sell low on emotion. Index funds work for most people precisely because they remove the temptation to make emotional decisions — broad diversification, automated discipline, and compound returns doing the quiet work. Becoming wealthy requires accepting that you are not special, you cannot predict the future, and boring consistency beats exciting speculation every time.
